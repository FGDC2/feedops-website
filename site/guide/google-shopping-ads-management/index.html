Google Shopping guide

Google Shopping Ads Management Guide 2026

Last updated: 17 June 2026

Google Shopping ads management works best when campaigns are built around business goals, not default platform prompts. Start with margin, stock, customer intent, and feed quality, then choose the campaign structure and bidding strategy that supports the commercial outcome.

The framework

Manage Shopping ads by connecting business goals to product-level execution.

The best Shopping campaigns are not simply PMax or Standard Shopping campaigns. They are commercial systems: clear objectives, clean product data, sensible segmentation, controlled bidding, and regular product-level review.

1. Define the business goal

Decide whether the campaign is built for revenue, margin, clearance, new customers, store visits, or category growth before touching Google Ads settings.

2. Prepare the feed

Shopping ads depend on product data. Fix titles, attributes, product types, availability, pricing, images, and custom labels before expecting automation to perform.

3. Choose campaign structure

Use Performance Max for scale and automated reach. Use Standard Shopping when you need tighter testing, clearer budget isolation, or stronger product-level control.

4. Set bids and budgets

Move from data collection to Target ROAS or profit-led targets once conversion volume is stable and tracking is trustworthy.

5. Review product performance

Find products with no impressions, spend without sales, high clicks with low conversion, or strong returns that deserve more budget.

6. Label dynamically

Use feed-driven custom labels to group products by ROAS band, zombie status, margin tier, stock pressure, and optimisation priority.

Step 1

Start with the business outcome, not Google’s default settings.

The biggest mistake in Google Shopping ads management is starting with the campaign type Google recommends before defining the business result. Google can optimize toward conversion value, but it does not automatically know your margin, stock pressure, retail calendar, priority categories, or offline sales goals.

Before creating or changing campaigns, define what the campaign is supposed to do. A clearance push needs a different structure from a high-margin evergreen campaign. A store-visit objective needs a different product and location strategy from an online-only revenue target.

Common Shopping campaign goals

  • Maximize revenue from top-selling products.
  • Protect margin on premium or high-value categories.
  • Clear seasonal, old, or overstocked inventory.
  • Increase visibility for new product ranges.
  • Drive store visits with local inventory and location signals.
  • Acquire new customers without sacrificing profitable repeat demand.

Those goals should flow back into the feed. If the goal is margin protection, the feed needs reliable margin or margin-tier labels. If the goal is clearance, the feed needs stock age, stock level, sale status, or markdown signals. If the goal is new customer acquisition, the feed may need labels that separate hero products, entry-level products, and repeat-purchase products.

This is why Shopping management and feed management should not be treated as separate jobs. Campaigns decide how Google spends. The feed decides what Google understands, what it can segment, and which products can be grouped into meaningful campaigns.

Management principle: If the goal is unclear, automation will usually chase the easiest conversion path rather than the most valuable business outcome.

Step 2

Choose Performance Max, Standard Shopping, or both based on control needs.

Performance Max and Standard Shopping are not opposing strategies. They solve different management problems. Performance Max is useful when you want Google to optimize across Shopping, Search, Display, YouTube, Discover, Gmail, and Maps from a shared asset and product base. Standard Shopping is useful when you need clearer product-level control and testing.

Performance Max Best for scale, automation, multi-channel reach, new customer goals, local inventory, and campaigns where you have enough conversion data for machine learning.
Standard Shopping Best for controlled testing, product group control, clearer search term visibility through reporting workflows, budget isolation, and protecting strategically important products.
Hybrid structure Use PMax for broad demand capture and Standard Shopping for specific product sets, tests, clearance groups, or categories where you need extra control.

Many accounts perform best with a hybrid structure. The key is to avoid letting campaigns compete chaotically. Segment by business goal, product value, stock status, margin, or test objective so each campaign has a clear job.

Example hybrid Shopping structure

  • Core PMax campaign: Contains proven products with stable conversion history, strong feed quality, and enough volume for automation.
  • High-margin Standard Shopping campaign: Protects products where profitability matters more than raw conversion volume.
  • Clearance campaign: Uses sale, stock age, or markdown labels from the feed so old inventory can be pushed without distorting the main campaign target.
  • Testing campaign: Holds new products, recently optimised products, or products coming out of a zombie review so you can watch whether feed changes improve visibility and click quality.

The structure should follow the labels and product facts in the feed. If your campaign names say "high margin" but the feed has no margin tier, the structure will eventually drift. If your campaigns are based on product type, but product types are inconsistent, reporting becomes unreliable. For product type cleanup, use the Product Type Google Shopping explainer, then connect the cleanup to the broader Google Shopping Feed Optimization Guide.

Step 3

Make the product feed strong enough for automation to learn correctly.

Shopping ads are feed-led. Google builds the ad from your product data and uses that data to understand what each product is, which searches it should match, and how attractive it may be to shoppers. Poor product data creates poor campaign learning.

Feed fields to review before scaling

  • Product titles: Include the product type, brand, key attributes, model, size, color, material, or compatibility where relevant.
  • Product types: Use a consistent hierarchy that supports reporting, segmentation, and long-tail relevance.
  • Attributes: Fill category-specific attributes that help Google classify and filter products, especially where Google Product Category accuracy affects campaign grouping or Merchant Center checks.
  • Images: Use clean, accurate images that show the actual product clearly.
  • Price and availability: Keep offer data aligned with the landing page to avoid disapprovals and wasted clicks.
  • Custom labels: Add management labels for margin, seasonality, stock level, priority, clearance, or performance state.

A feed audit should be part of campaign management, not a separate technical chore. Feed quality directly affects eligibility, matching, CTR, conversion rate, and how efficiently Performance Max learns.

Feed quality also affects the management decisions you make later. If titles are vague, a low CTR may not mean the product is unwanted. It may mean Google is matching it to the wrong searches or showing a listing that does not communicate the product clearly. If product types are shallow, your campaign reports may group unlike products together. If attributes are missing, free listings, Shopping filters, and PMax matching can all suffer.

For product titles, the most useful management question is not "Can we add more keywords?" It is "Does this title tell Google and the shopper what the product is, why it is relevant, and which variant it represents?" For title examples and templates, use the Google Shopping Title Optimization Guide.

For deeper feed work, read the Google Shopping Feed Optimization Guide.

Step 4

Use segmentation to make budget and reporting decisions easier.

Campaign segmentation should make management clearer. If every product sits in one campaign with one target, you may lose sight of which categories are carrying performance, which products are wasting spend, and which priorities are being underfunded.

Useful segmentation signals

  • Margin tier: Protect high-margin products from the same targets as low-margin volume drivers.
  • Stock position: Push overstock, reduce pressure on low stock, and avoid advertising unavailable products.
  • Seasonality: Separate short seasonal windows from evergreen categories.
  • Lifecycle: Treat new, mature, clearance, and discontinued products differently.
  • Performance state: Identify products with no impressions, no clicks, spend without return, or strong return on ad spend.

Good segmentation gives each campaign one job. Poor segmentation creates mixed signals. For example, if a campaign contains high-margin products, low-margin volume products, clearance products, and new untested products, a single ROAS target will not be right for the whole group. Google may spend where conversions are easiest, while the business needed margin protection or stock clearance.

The feed is the cleanest place to store segmentation logic because it travels with the SKU. Product labels can be built from product type, price band, margin tier, stock level, sale status, season, brand, or performance data. That makes the labels useful for campaigns, reports, exclusions, and automated rules.

FeedOps workflow: Custom labels can be managed from the feed so campaign structures and reports stay aligned with real product data instead of manual spreadsheets.

Step 5

Use dynamic custom labelling for ROAS bands and campaign control.

Dynamic custom labelling is one of the most practical ways to connect feed data with Shopping campaign management. Instead of manually moving products between campaigns, you use rules to assign labels based on performance, margin, stock, product type, or commercial priority. Those labels can then drive campaign inclusion, reporting, bidding, and review workflows.

A common use case is ROAS banding. Products performing above one ROAS threshold can sit in a campaign with a more ambitious growth target. Products in a middle ROAS band can sit in a stable efficiency campaign. Products below the target can sit in a lower-budget optimisation or recovery campaign until the feed, offer, price, or landing page is improved.

Example ROAS band labels

ROAS high Products above target ROAS, with enough clicks and conversions to trust the result. These may deserve more budget, a growth campaign, or a less restrictive target.
ROAS stable Products near target ROAS. These usually belong in the core campaign where the goal is controlled, predictable efficiency.
ROAS low Products below target but still getting meaningful traffic. These need diagnosis before you cut them: feed quality, price, offer, competition, landing page, or channel fit.
No data Products without enough impressions, clicks, or conversions to judge. These should not be treated the same as genuinely poor performers.

Why separate ROAS bands into campaigns

ROAS bands are most useful when they shape campaign strategy. A high-ROAS product group can be placed into a campaign with a different budget and target from a low-ROAS product group. This is campaign-level separation, not ad group separation. The point is to give Google a cleaner budget, target, and learning environment for products with similar commercial behaviour.

For example, a retailer might run one campaign for products consistently above 700 percent ROAS, another for products between 350 and 700 percent ROAS, and a recovery campaign for products below 350 percent ROAS. The exact bands depend on margin and business model. A 400 percent ROAS may be excellent for one category and unprofitable for another.

Rules that make dynamic labels safer

  • Use enough data: Do not move products based on one click or one conversion. Set minimum impression, click, cost, and conversion thresholds.
  • Use a lookback window: A 30, 60, or 90 day window can smooth out noisy daily changes.
  • Combine ROAS with margin: A high-ROAS low-margin product may be less valuable than a lower-ROAS high-margin product.
  • Exclude unavailable products: Stock status and availability should override performance labels.
  • Keep a testing label: Recently optimised products need time to prove whether feed changes improved performance.
Feed connection: Dynamic labels are only as useful as the feed data behind them. ROAS bands become stronger when combined with product type, margin tier, title quality, attribute completeness, stock status, and promotion status.

Step 6

Set bidding targets according to data maturity and profitability.

Bidding should reflect how much trustworthy conversion data the account has and what the business can afford. A new or unstable campaign may need a learning period before aggressive Target ROAS constraints are useful. A mature campaign can usually move toward tighter ROAS or profit-led targets.

A practical bidding progression

  • Start with clean tracking: Confirm conversion value, revenue, tax, shipping, refunds, and offline actions are handled properly.
  • Collect signal: Use a strategy that allows enough volume for the campaign to learn.
  • Move to Target ROAS: Apply targets once conversion volume is stable and the campaign is not starved of data.
  • Refine by segment: Use different targets for different margin, category, stock, or growth priorities.
  • Review incrementally: Avoid large target changes that reset learning or cut off profitable volume too quickly.

ROAS is useful, but it is not the whole story. A high ROAS campaign can still underfund growth. A lower ROAS campaign can be valuable if it clears stock, brings in new customers, or drives store visits with acceptable margin.

When ROAS bands are managed through dynamic custom labels, bidding becomes less blunt. Instead of forcing every product into one target, you can use different campaign targets for different product groups. A high-margin, high-ROAS campaign may be able to scale. A low-ROAS recovery campaign may need a lower budget, a lower target during testing, or a strict target while feed improvements are made.

Do not change targets in isolation. If a product is underperforming because the title is weak, the category is wrong, or attributes are missing, a bid change is treating the symptom. Fix the feed first, then let the campaign retest the product under a clear label such as recently optimised or title improved.

Step 7

Use promotions and offer signals to improve competitiveness.

Promotions can improve Shopping visibility, click-through rate, and conversion rate when they are relevant and well timed. They are especially useful for competitive categories, seasonal campaigns, and products that get impressions but do not earn enough clicks.

When promotions help

  • Products have impressions but weak click-through rate.
  • Competitors are more attractive on price or delivery.
  • You need to clear old or seasonal stock.
  • You are launching a new product range and need early traction.
  • You want to support a retail event without changing the whole campaign structure.

Promotions work best when they are coordinated with product feed data, landing page messaging, stock levels, and campaign budgets. A promotion that drives clicks to low-stock or low-margin products can look successful in Google Ads while hurting the business.

Promotion strategy should also use labels. Products can be labelled as sale, clearance, bundle eligible, free shipping eligible, high stock, or seasonal. Those labels help separate genuine promotional opportunities from products that need feed optimisation first. If a product has poor CTR because the title is vague, a discount may hide the problem instead of fixing it.

Step 8

Measure product-level performance, not just campaign averages.

Campaign averages can hide important product problems. A strong overall ROAS may conceal products spending without return, categories with no visibility, or high-margin SKUs that are being underfunded. Shopping management should include regular product-level reviews.

Signals to monitor

  • Products with no impressions and no clicks.
  • Products with impressions but no clicks.
  • Products with clicks and spend but no conversions.
  • Products with high conversion value that are limited by budget or low impression share.
  • Products with strong ROAS but low margin.
  • Products with weak feed attributes, disapprovals, or landing page mismatch.

Use product-level findings to decide the next action. Some products need feed work. Some need pricing or promotional support. Some need a different bidding target. Others should be excluded, deprioritized, or grouped into a clearance strategy.

The most useful report is a product action report, not just a performance report. It should tell you what to do next: optimise the title, improve product type, backfill attributes, check the landing page, review price competitiveness, add a promotion, move the product into a different ROAS band, or exclude it from a campaign.

Paid Shopping reports should also be compared with organic Shopping visibility. If products earn free listing impressions but struggle in paid campaigns, the issue may be bidding, ROAS targets, or campaign structure. If products struggle in both, the feed usually needs deeper work. Use the Google Shopping Free Listings Guide to review the organic Shopping side.

Step 9

Check zombie products and make sure they are zombies for the right reason.

Zombie products are products that sit in the feed but do not contribute meaningfully. They may get no impressions, no clicks, no conversions, or spend without return. The management mistake is assuming every zombie product is bad. Some are bad products for the channel. Some are products nobody wants. Others are only zombies because the feed is not giving Google enough useful information.

Before excluding zombie products, diagnose why they are zombies. A product with no impressions may have an eligibility problem, weak product type, missing attributes, poor title, low demand, or it may be suppressed by campaign structure. A product with impressions but no clicks may have a weak title, poor image, unattractive price, unclear offer, or a search mismatch. A product with clicks but no conversions may have landing page, price, availability, delivery, or product-market fit issues.

Three reasons a product becomes a zombie

Nobody wants it The product has weak demand, poor price competitiveness, limited appeal, or is out of season. Feed work may not solve the commercial problem.
Wrong for the channel The product may sell through another channel, but not through Google Shopping. This can happen with complex, highly considered, restricted, niche, or low-visual products.
Not properly optimised The product has weak titles, missing attributes, poor product type, bad images, category issues, or feed data that prevents Google and shoppers from understanding it.

Zombie product workflow

  1. Check eligibility: Confirm the product is approved, in stock, correctly priced, and eligible for the intended destination.
  2. Check visibility: Separate products with no impressions from products with impressions but no clicks.
  3. Check feed quality: Review title, product type, attributes, image, description, GTIN, availability, and landing page consistency.
  4. Check channel fit: Ask whether the product is likely to sell through Google Shopping or whether another channel is more suitable.
  5. Optimise and relabel: If feed issues are found, improve the product and add a dynamic label such as recently optimised or zombie recovery.
  6. Retest before excluding: Give optimised products a clean testing window before deciding they are genuinely not worth advertising.

If the diagnosis is feed quality, start with the Google Shopping Feed Optimization Guide. If the main weakness is the product title, use the Google Shopping Title Optimization Guide. If the product has been optimised and still gets no meaningful demand, it may be better to exclude it, lower its priority, or move it to a small testing campaign rather than letting it distort the main campaign.

Useful label: Add a zombie reason or zombie status custom label. Examples include no demand, wrong channel, title issue, attribute issue, price issue, recently optimised, and exclude candidate.

Checklist

Google Shopping ads management checklist.

  1. Define the business goal before choosing campaign settings.
  2. Confirm conversion tracking and conversion value are accurate.
  3. Audit the product feed for titles, attributes, product types, images, price, availability, and Merchant Center issues.
  4. Add custom labels for margin, stock, priority, seasonality, clearance, and performance state.
  5. Create dynamic custom labels for ROAS bands, including high ROAS, stable ROAS, low ROAS, and no-data products.
  6. Separate ROAS bands into campaigns when they need different budgets, targets, or testing rules.
  7. Choose Performance Max, Standard Shopping, or a hybrid structure based on control needs.
  8. Set bidding targets according to data maturity and commercial margin.
  9. Use promotions where they support product competitiveness and stock goals.
  10. Review product-level performance, not only campaign-level ROAS.
  11. Identify products with no impressions, weak CTR, spend without return, or strong growth potential.
  12. Diagnose zombie products before excluding them: no demand, wrong channel, or not properly optimised.
  13. Link zombie recovery work back to feed optimisation, title optimisation, product type cleanup, and attribute backfill.
  14. Repeat feed, campaign, and product-level reviews on a regular cadence.

FAQ

Google Shopping ads management FAQs.

What is Google Shopping ads management?

Google Shopping ads management is the process of planning, running, optimizing, and reporting on Shopping campaigns in Google Ads. It covers business goals, feed quality, campaign structure, bidding, promotions, and product-level performance.

What is the difference between Performance Max and Standard Shopping?

Performance Max uses automation across Google channels, including Shopping, Search, YouTube, Display, Discover, Gmail, and Maps. Standard Shopping gives more direct control over product groups, bids, testing, and budget isolation.

Should I run Performance Max and Standard Shopping together?

Often, yes. A hybrid structure can work well when Performance Max handles broad automated reach and Standard Shopping handles specific product groups, tests, clearance campaigns, or categories that need tighter control.

Why is the product feed important for Google Shopping ads?

Google Shopping ads are created and matched from feed data. Product titles, attributes, product types, images, price, availability, and custom labels affect eligibility, relevance, CTR, conversion rate, and campaign learning.

What are custom labels used for?

Custom labels help segment products by margin, seasonality, stock level, product priority, clearance status, or performance state. They make campaign structure, bidding, and reporting easier to manage.

What is dynamic custom labelling in Google Shopping?

Dynamic custom labelling uses feed rules or product data workflows to update labels automatically based on ROAS band, margin, stock, sale status, product type, or performance state. These labels can then be used to group products into the right Shopping campaigns.

Should ROAS bands be separated by campaign?

Often, yes. Products in different ROAS bands may need different budgets, targets, and testing rules. High-ROAS products can sit in a growth campaign, stable products can sit in a core campaign, and low-ROAS products can sit in a recovery or optimisation campaign.

How should I set Google Shopping bidding targets?

Start with accurate conversion tracking, collect enough data for the campaign to learn, then move toward Target ROAS or profit-led targets. Targets should reflect margin, stock, category value, and growth goals.

Do promotions improve Shopping ads performance?

Promotions can improve visibility, click-through rate, and conversion rate when they match the product, stock position, landing page, and commercial goal. They are most useful when competitiveness or urgency matters.

What should I review in Shopping campaign reports?

Review ROAS, conversion value, margin, CTR, impression share, new customer value, store visits, products with no visibility, products with spend but no sales, and products that deserve more budget.

What are zombie products in Google Shopping?

Zombie products are products that get no meaningful Shopping performance. They may have no impressions, no clicks, no conversions, or spend without return. Before excluding them, check whether the issue is lack of demand, poor channel fit, or weak feed optimisation.

How do I fix zombie products?

Start by checking eligibility, feed quality, product titles, product types, attributes, images, price, availability, and landing page consistency. If feed issues are found, optimise the product and relabel it for retesting before deciding whether to exclude it.

How often should Google Shopping campaigns be reviewed?

Check feed health and spend regularly, then run deeper product-level reviews weekly, fortnightly, or monthly depending on catalogue size, SKU changes, budget, and category competitiveness.

Can a Google Shopping agency still add value when bidding is automated?

Yes. Automation handles execution, but strategy still matters. Strong management improves goals, data inputs, feed quality, structure, targets, promotions, reporting, and the actions taken after performance changes.
